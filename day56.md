A recommendation is only valuable if it's honest.

Sometimes, the best answer isn't **"This is perfect."**

It's **"This is the closest match—and here's why."**

That became today's biggest lesson.

For **Day 56/60** of my ABTalks 60-Day Claude AI Mastery Challenge—and **Day 6 of building TeeTronics Gadgets Smart Match**—I built the heart of the entire product: the recommendation engine.

A customer answers seven simple questions about their budget, intended use, and preferred features.

Behind the scenes, the app evaluates all **25 laptops** in the catalog, filters them by budget, scores each one against the customer's priorities, and recommends the strongest match—with a clear explanation written in plain language.

Not just *what* to buy.

But *why*.

The biggest surprise wasn't getting the scoring algorithm to work.

It was handling the impossible requests.

What happens when someone wants a touchscreen...

A fingerprint sensor...

16GB RAM...

An Intel Core i7...

...while working with the smallest possible budget?

Many recommendation systems either fail quietly...

...or pretend they've found a perfect match.

I wanted Smart Match to do something different.

Tell the truth.

Instead of forcing an unrealistic recommendation, the app explains that no laptop meets every requirement, recommends the closest available option, and clearly points out the compromises.

That decision changed how I think about product design.

Because trust isn't built when everything goes right.

It's built when your product is honest about its limitations.

The biggest lesson I took away from today's build wasn't about algorithms.

It was about credibility.

**People don't expect every product to have the perfect answer.**

**They do expect it to tell the truth.**

A recommendation engine earns trust not by sounding confident...

...but by being transparent when confidence isn't justified.

By the end of today, Smart Match became a complete working MVP.

A real laptop catalog.

A guided recommendation flow.

A live scoring engine.

Transparent reasoning.

A comparison view.

Tested across realistic, low-budget, and even impossible buying scenarios.
